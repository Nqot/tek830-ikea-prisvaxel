Challenge 1. Make sustainable affordability the engine of growth 
IKEA’s business idea depends on offering well-designed, functional products at prices so low 
that the many people can afford them, while its scale and franchise model support continued 
growth. Recent price reductions increased sales quantities, showing how affordability can drive 
higher volumes. The strategic challenge is to make healthy and sustainable choices genuinely 
affordable and attractive while halving absolute value-chain emissions by FY30 and reducing 
resource use even as the business grows. This is critical because success cannot be measured 
only per product: total growth must remain within IKEA’s absolute climate and resource 
commitments.  
 
In simple terms: 
 How can IKEA keep prices low and attract more customers without increasing its total 
environmental impact? 
The tension: 
• Lower prices = more sales 
• More sales = more products produced and transported 
• More products can mean higher emissions and resource use 
 
Problem Statement 
How can IKEA make sustainable choices the most affordable choice while continuing to grow 
and reducing total climate impact? 
 
Proto Persona 
 
• Emma, 32, Young Family Planner 
 Wants sustainable products but has a limited household budget. 
• Markus, 25, Cost-Conscious Shopper 
 Prioritizes the lowest price and wants help finding affordable sustainable options.  
• Johan, 50, Durability-Focused Shopper 
 Believes buying fewer, longer-lasting products is more sustainable and wants products 
that offer long-term value. 
• Elin, 38, Design-Oriented Customer 
 Values aesthetics and quality but wants to make responsible purchasing decisions. 
 
Data opportunity: 
Track which products, materials, and customer choices create the biggest sustainability / cost / 
design / quality impact while remaining affordable. 
 
 
Challenge 2. Secure a resilient, regenerative and traceable material system

Materials are the largest part of the IKEA climate footprint, while the business depends heavily 
on forests, agriculture, water, minerals and functioning ecosystems. IKEA must secure 
affordable, high-volume access to low-carbon, renewable and recycled materials while 
preventing deforestation, reducing water withdrawal, minimising harmful chemicals and 
respecting people connected to sourcing. This requires much stronger traceability and place-
specific action, because ecosystem disruption can create material shortages, price increases 
and compliance costs. Material transformation is therefore not only a sustainability priority; it is 
fundamental to future product availability, cost and business resilience.  
 
In simple terms: 
 How can IKEA know exactly where its materials come from and ensure they are sustainable, 
available, and affordable in the future? 
The tension: 
• IKEA relies heavily on wood, cotton, metals, water, and other natural resources. 
• Climate change, deforestation, and resource shortages can disrupt supply and increase 
costs. 
Problem Statement 
 How can IKEA use data to create full visibility of materials, ensuring they are low-carbon, 
responsibly sourced, and resilient to future supply risks? 
 
Focus Areas (You can choose one focus area to work with proto persona) 
• Wood 
• Metal 
• Plastics 
• Textiles 
 
Proto Persona 
Johan, 41, Material Sourcing Leader 
Needs better insight into material origins, environmental impacts, and supply chain risks to 
make smarter sourcing decisions.  
 
Data opportunity: 
 Provide visibility into material sources, environmental risks, supplier performance, and future 
shortages. 
 
 
Challenge 3. Turn circularity into a scalable and profitable business model 
IKEA aims for a 90% average circular fulfilment score, at least 90% recycled or renewable 
content and circular services in at least 80% of markets by 2030. The challenge is to connect 
circular product design with affordable secondary materials, repair and spare parts, take-back, 
resale, reverse logistics and convenient customer experiences—rather than treating circularity 
as a separate service layer. Current IKEA reporting identifies regulatory, infrastructure, logistics, 
customer-expectation and capability barriers to scaling these models. Solving them is essential 
to decouple growth from virgin material use and end-of-life waste while creating new forms of 
customer value.

In simple terms: 
How can IKEA make repairing, reusing, reselling, and recycling products as easy and attractive 
as buying new ones? 
 
The tension: 
• Circularity is as important contribution for sustainability. 
• It is often harder, more expensive, and less convenient than buying new. 
 
Problem Statement 
How can IKEA make repair, reuse, resale, and recycling easier, more convenient, and more 
profitable than buying new products? 
 
Possible Focus Areas (choose one): 
• Repair: Making repairs and spare parts more accessible and attractive. 
• Resale / Recommerce: Helping customers buy and sell pre-owned IKEA products. 
• Take-back: Improving product return and collection programs. 
• Recycling: Increasing material recovery and reducing waste. 
• Recirculation: Keeping products in use for as long as possible through reuse and 
redistribution. 
• Reverse Logistics: Optimizing the flow of returned products and materials. 
 
 
Consider both perspectives 
• The customer buying a used, repaired, or refurbished product. 
• The customer selling, returning, repairing, or recycling a product. 
 
Proto Persona 
Sara, 28, Urban Renter 
Frequently moves homes and wants affordable, flexible ways to buy, repair, sell, or return 
furniture. 
 
Data opportunity: 
Use data to optimize take-back programs, resale platforms, repair services, spare parts 
availability, and reverse logistics. 
 
 
Challenge 4. Deliver a fair and just transition across the value chain 
IKEA’s value chain spans external suppliers, manufacturing, transport, independent franchisees 
and retail markets; approximately 90% of the product range is sourced from external suppliers. 
IKEA has identified potential health and safety risks, forced-labor and child-labor concerns as 
material value-chain impacts, while the strategy commits to stronger due diligence, decent 
work, living wages and a just transition. The challenge is to turn these commitments into 
prevention, worker engagement, remedy and measurable improvement across multiple tiers 
and geographies—also where climate, circularity and technology change jobs and livelihoods. 
This is critical for resilience, trust, regulatory compliance and IKEA’s license to operate.

In simple terms: 
How can IKEA make sure people across its global supply chain are treated fairly and work in safe 
conditions? 
 
The tension: 
• IKEA works with thousands of suppliers worldwide. 
• Risks such as poor working conditions, low wages, or labor rights violations can exist 
deep in the supply chain. 
 
Problem Statement 
 How can IKEA use data to identify, prevent, and improve social and labor risks across its global 
value chain?
